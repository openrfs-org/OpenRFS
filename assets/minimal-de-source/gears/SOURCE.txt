The root window is glxgears, and the geometry is Brian Paul's.

Nothing is vendored here.  tools/make-gears.py carries the numbers out
of Mesa's own demo and projects them into the outlines the shell fills;
src/trait_gears_art.h is the result, and src/gears.c draws it.

What was taken, and it is the arithmetic rather than any code:

    gear(inner, outer, width, teeth, tooth_depth) builds a gear whose
    root radius is outer - tooth_depth/2 and whose tip radius is
    outer + tooth_depth/2, with da = 2*pi/teeth/4 and a tooth that runs
    r1 -> r2 -> r2 -> r1 across angle, +da, +2da, +3da.

    gear1 = gear(1.0, 4.0, 1.0, 20, 0.7)   red     at (-3.0, -2.0)
    gear2 = gear(0.5, 2.0, 2.0, 10, 0.7)   green   at ( 3.1, -2.0)
    gear3 = gear(1.3, 2.0, 0.5, 10, 0.7)   blue    at (-3.1,  4.2)

    view_rotx 20, view_roty 30, view_rotz 0, and the two small gears
    turned -9 and -25 degrees so the teeth mesh.

Two things are deliberately not carried over.

The projection is orthographic where glxgears' is perspective.  The
scene is about thirteen units across seen from forty away, so the two
differ by a few percent; what the change buys is that a flat extrusion
projects to one outline swept along one vector, which is a scanline fill
rather than a mesh, and this desktop has no floating point to do a mesh
with.

The lighting is gone.  glxgears lights every face from (5, 5, 10) and
gets a continuous range of shades; there are sixteen colours here.  Each
gear is two of them - the face the light reaches and the sides it does
not - which is what flat shading leaves when the shades run out.
